Two readings matter more than the rest of the table. The 900-1,000 MPa compressive figure is a material capacity, not an allowable: glazing fails in tension at surface flaws, at 30-50 MPa when annealed, so the tensile rows are the ones that govern sizing. Density and unit weight are fixed by composition alone — heat-strengthened and tempered glass carry exactly the same 2,500 kg/m³ and 24.5 kN/m³, and only the strength rows change. How compression and tension come to differ by a factor of twenty, and what each test condition means, is covered under Mechanical Properties.

## Overview

Soda-lime glass is the most common form of glass (~90% of all glass produced), composed of approximately 70-75% silica (SiO₂), 12-15% soda (Na₂O), and 8-12% lime (CaO). It is optically transparent, inexpensive, chemically inert, and easily formed — the material of windows, bottles, jars, and light bulbs. As an amorphous ceramic, it exhibits brittle fracture at room temperature but becomes viscous above its glass transition temperature (~550°C).

<Formula>ρ = 2500 kg/m³ (2.5 g/cm³) — higher than most polymers and light metals, lower than steel</Formula>

## Soda Lime Glass Composition

Soda-lime glass is a three-component system — silica, soda and lime — with small additions that fine-tune melting behaviour and durability. The mass fractions below are typical commercial windows for float and container glass, not unique values: individual plants and product families shift within these ranges, and the approximate figures quoted in the Overview above sit inside them.

| Oxide | Typical content (mass %) | Role in the glass |
|---|---|---|
| SiO₂ (silica) | 70-75 (container glass is often quoted lower, at 70-74) | Network former — builds the amorphous silica framework |
| Na₂O (soda) | 12-16 | Flux — breaks silica bridges and drops the melting temperature |
| CaO (lime) | 5-12 | Stabiliser — restores the durability that soda takes away |
| MgO (magnesia) | 0-4 | Stabiliser — lowers viscosity at forming temperatures |
| Al₂O₃ (alumina) | 0-2 | Improves chemical durability, suppresses devitrification |
| K₂O (potash) | 0-1 | Flux — fine-tunes expansion and working range |
| Fe₂O₃ (iron oxide) | below 0.5 | Colorant — green tint; low-iron grades for clarity |
| SO₃ and other refining agents | below 1 | Fining — remove seed bubbles during melting |

Two of these roles decide most of the engineering behaviour. Silica is the network former: it supplies the continuous random tetrahedral framework that gives glass its hardness, its 70 GPa modulus and its transparency. Soda is the flux that makes the batch melt at around 1500°C instead of the far higher temperature pure silica demands, but each sodium ion sits in the structure as a non-bridging modifier that opens it up and weakens it. Lime is the compensator: calcium ions tighten the network back, which is why the three oxides are used together — a silica-soda binary is water-soluble and useless as a container material.

Composition, not heat treatment, sets density. Sodium and calcium ions occupy the interstices of the silica framework and are denser per unit volume than the voids they fill, which is why adding them takes the solid from roughly 2,200 kg/m³ for pure fused silica to 2,500 kg/m³ for soda-lime. Substituting boron oxide for part of the soda and lime reverses the effect: borosilicate glass lands at 2,230 kg/m³ through a similar processing route. That is why density works as a composition fingerprint on this page, while tempering leaves it untouched — annealed and tempered soda-lime glass are both 2,500 kg/m³.

Composition also fixes chemical durability, and here the soda content is the weak point. Water and acids exchange with the sodium ions at the surface, while strong alkali attacks the silica network outright, so soda-lime glass is the wrong choice for caustic service, for hydrofluoric acid, and for repeated hot alkaline washing. Raising lime and alumina, or replacing part of the soda with boron oxide, buys durability at higher cost — which is exactly why laboratory and process sight glass moves to borosilicate while windows and bottles stay on soda-lime. The oxide-by-oxide comparison against borosilicate and fused silica is in the Composition Comparison table further down this page.

### Compressive Strength of Soda-Lime Glass (900-1000 MPa, Typical Values)

When a soda-lime specimen is loaded in pure compression, failure occurs at 900-1000 MPa — roughly twenty times the strength the same glass shows in tension, and several times the compressive capacity of structural steel or an aluminium alloy on the same area basis. The figure is a genuine material property, reproducible across annealed, heat-strengthened and tempered glass, because all three share the same silica network.

The spread within that 900-1000 MPa band is a test artefact rather than a material variation. Compression testing of glass loads a small prism or cylinder between flat, hardened platens with the end faces ground parallel; the measured stress then depends on specimen slenderness, on friction between platen and glass (which prevents the specimen from expanding laterally and inflates the apparent strength), and on how squarely the load is applied. Cushioning the ends or changing the height-to-width ratio moves the result within the range, which is why test programmes report a distribution rather than a single allowable number. Note also that glazing codes do not qualify structural glass by a compression test at all — bending tests are specified instead, because that is how glazing actually fails in service.

The gap against tensile strength comes from how a crack responds to the sign of the stress. Glass carries surface microcracks from cutting, handling and grinding. Under tension those cracks open and concentrate stress at their tips, and with no plastic zone to blunt them they run at a stress far below the theoretical strength of the bonds — the Griffith criterion — so the practical tensile limit is set by the worst flaw present, not by the bulk material. Under compression the same cracks are squeezed shut and cannot propagate; the glass can then only fail by shear or crushing, which requires the full 900-1000 MPa. That asymmetry is a direct consequence of brittleness: a ductile metal redistributes a local overload by yielding, while glass has no such mechanism.

Heat treatment does not raise compressive strength, and this is where tempered glass is widely misread. Tempering quenches the surface into residual compression and leaves the core in tension. That pre-compression opposes the tensile stress a bending load produces, so usable tensile strength climbs from 30-50 MPa to 120-200 MPa — the factor of three to four noted in the type comparison below. Set against a compressive capacity of 900-1000 MPa, however, the residual surface stress is small, so the compression figure is essentially unchanged. Tempering buys tensile performance, not compressive performance.

For design the rule follows from the same asymmetry: glass is never sized on compressive strength. Panels, fins and overhead glazing fail in bending, where the tensile face reaches its flaw-limited strength long before the compressive face comes anywhere near its capacity. Compressive strength becomes the governing value only in direct bearing — glass columns, glass fins seated in shoes, bearing pads and point-supported fittings — and even there the allowable contact stress is held well below 900-1000 MPa to account for edge condition, local crushing and stress concentration at the contact patch. Where glass bears on a support, check the bearing detail rather than the material data sheet.

## Specific Gravity

Specific gravity (SG) is the ratio of a material's density to water at 4°C (1000 kg/m³):

<Formula>SG = ρ_glass / ρ_water = 2500 / 1000 = 2.5</Formula>

Soda-lime glass has SG ≈ 2.5 — about 2.5x heavier than the same volume of water, similar to aluminum (2.7) and lighter than steel (7.85). Borosilicate glass is slightly lighter (SG ≈ 2.23), while fused silica is ~2.20. Specific gravity is used to convert between weight and volume in handling, packaging, and structural design calculations.

## Unit Weight

Unit weight (weight density) is the weight per unit volume — density multiplied by gravitational acceleration:

<Formula>γ = ρ × g = 2500 kg/m³ × 9.81 m/s² ≈ 24.5 kN/m³ (156 lb/ft³)</Formula>

| Glass Type | Unit Weight |
|---|---|
| Soda-lime | 24.5 kN/m³ (156 lb/ft³) |
| Borosilicate (Pyrex) | 21.9 kN/m³ (139 lb/ft³) |
| Fused silica | 21.6 kN/m³ (137 lb/ft³) |
| Tempered (same composition) | 24.5 kN/m³ (156 lb/ft³) |

Use unit weight when calculating glass panel dead loads, glazing support reactions, and shipping/transport weights.

## Density and Strength by Glass Type

Density is set by composition, while strength is set by composition, surface condition and heat treatment. The two are often confused: tempering raises the usable tensile strength by 3-4 times without changing density at all, because the silica network is the same — only the residual stress pattern differs. The table below keeps both numbers side by side so panel weight and allowable stress can be read from one place.

| Glass type | Density (kg/m³) | Compressive strength (MPa) | Design tensile (MPa) | Young's modulus (GPa) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Soda-lime, annealed | 2,500 | 900-1,000 | 30-50 | 70 |
| Soda-lime, heat-strengthened | 2,500 | 900-1,000 | 60-90 | 70 |
| Soda-lime, tempered (toughened) | 2,500 | 900-1,000 | 120-200 | 70 |
| Laminated (annealed glass layers) | 2,500 | 900-1,000 | 30-50 per layer | 70 |
| Borosilicate (Pyrex) | 2,230 | 900-1,000 | 60-70 | 64 |
| Fused silica / quartz glass | 2,200 | about 1,100 | 50-70 | 72 |
| Aluminosilicate, chemically strengthened | 2,450 | 1,000+ | 400-700 | 75 |

Three practical readings from this table:

- **Density depends on the oxide mix, not the treatment.** Soda-lime, heat-strengthened and tempered glass are all 2,500 kg/m³ because they share the same 70-75% SiO₂ composition; only borosilicate (2,230 kg/m³) and fused silica (2,200 kg/m³) are lighter. A tempered 6 mm panel and an annealed 6 mm panel weigh the same 15 kg/m².
- **Compressive strength is not a design value for glass.** Although soda-lime glass reaches 900-1,000 MPa in compression, glazing almost never fails in compression — it fails in tension at surface flaws, at 30-50 MPa when annealed. The compressive number matters only where glass bears directly on a support, such as a glass column or a bearing block.
- **Tempered glass density is often quoted as 2,500 kg/m³.** Because the process changes no chemistry, the value used for weight takeoffs is the same as annealed soda-lime. Tempered glass is specified for its strength (120-200 MPa), not for its weight.

For a quick panel weight, multiply density by thickness: a 2,500 kg/m³ glass panel in 1 mm thickness is 2.5 kg/m², so 6 mm is 15 kg/m², 10 mm is 25 kg/m² and 12 mm is 30 kg/m². Borosilicate panels run about 11% lighter for the same thickness (2.23 kg/m² per mm).

### Composition Comparison: Soda-Lime vs Borosilicate vs Fused Silica

| Property | Soda-Lime | Borosilicate (Pyrex) | Fused Silica / Quartz |
|---|---|---|---|
| SiO₂ content | 70-75% | 80-81% | >99.9% |
| Density (kg/m³) | 2,500 | 2,230 | 2,200 |
| Thermal expansion (×10⁻⁶/°C) | 8-9 | 3.2-3.3 | 0.55 |
| Thermal conductivity (W/m·K) | 1.0 | 1.1 | 1.4 |
| Max continuous service temp | ~450°C | ~500°C | ~1000°C |
| Thermal shock resistance | Poor | Good | Excellent |
| Relative cost | Lowest | Moderate | Highest |
| Typical uses | Windows, bottles, containers | Labware, sight glasses, cookware | Optical fibers, quartz lamps, semiconductor |

Fused silica (also called quartz glass) is the highest-performance engineering glass: its near-zero thermal expansion (0.55 × 10⁻⁶/°C) makes it virtually immune to thermal shock, and it survives continuous service above 1000°C. It is the material of choice for optical fibers, high-temperature lamp envelopes, and semiconductor processing. Borosilicate is the practical middle ground for process sight glasses and laboratory ware.

## Applications

### Construction
- Float glass windows (flat glass produced by the Pilkington float process)
- Insulating glass units (double/triple glazing with argon fill)
- Tempered glass for doors, showers, railings (safety glazing)
- Laminated glass for skylights, hurricane zones, automotive windshields

### Packaging
- Bottles and jars (beverages, food, pharmaceuticals) — chemically inert, no flavor contamination
- Infinitely recyclable (re-melt without quality loss)

### Industrial
- Sight glasses in process vessels and piping (use borosilicate, not soda-lime, for thermal shock)
- Laboratory glassware (borosilicate — Pyrex/Kimax)
- Light bulbs (soda-lime for standard; fused silica for halogen/quartz-halogen)
- Tubing, rods, gauge glass

### Specialty
- Fiberglass (E-glass, S-glass for composites)
- Optical fibers (high-purity fused silica, different composition)
- Display glass (aluminosilicate, thin, chemically strengthened — Gorilla Glass class)

## Glass vs Steel vs Aluminum

| Property | Soda-Lime Glass | Structural Steel (A36) | Aluminum 6061-T6 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Density (kg/m³) | 2,500 | 7,850 | 2,700 |
| Young's Modulus (GPa) | 70 | 200 | 69 |
| Tensile Strength (MPa) | 30-50 (annealed) | 400-550 | 310 |
| Compressive Strength (MPa) | 900-1000 | ~250 (yield) | ~276 (yield) |
| Thermal Conductivity (W/m·K) | 1.0 | 50 | 167 |
| Thermal Expansion (×10⁻⁶/°C) | 8-9 | 12 | 23 |
| Fracture Behavior | Brittle (no yield) | Ductile | Ductile |
| Relative Cost | Low | Low | Moderate |

Glass is a thermal and electrical insulator (1.0 W/m·K vs 50 for steel and 167 for aluminum), which is why glazing dominates building heat loss. Its modulus (70 GPa) matches aluminum, but unlike metals it has zero plastic deformation — design must keep tensile stress below the flaw-limited strength. For weight-critical glazing, glass (2,500 kg/m³) is only slightly lighter than aluminum (2,700 kg/m³) and about one-third the weight of steel.

<Callout type="warning" title="Thermal Shock is a Glass Killer" severity="warning">
Soda-lime glass breaks from rapid temperature change because thermal gradients create differential expansion. The classic example: pouring boiling water into a cold glass bottle fails at stresses well below design strength. Borosilicate glass (Pyrex) has ~3x lower thermal expansion and resists thermal shock, making it the correct choice for sight glasses, laboratory ware, and any service involving rapid temperature changes.
</Callout>

## Design Considerations

- **Edge condition critical**: cut edges have microcracks that dominate strength; polished, seamed, or rounded edges much stronger
- **No plastic deformation**: glass fails catastrophically without warning (design tempered/laminated for safety-critical)
- **Static fatigue**: static load over time increases failure probability (delayed fracture from slow crack growth in humidity)
- **Design stress**: use 7-15 MPa allowable tensile for long-term annealed service (safety factor of 3-5)
- **Never load glass in bending from the edge**

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the density of glass?**
Soda-lime glass (the common window/bottle type) has a density of 2500 kg/m³ (2.5 g/cm³). Borosilicate glass is 2230 kg/m³ and fused silica 2200 kg/m³.

**What is the specific gravity of glass?**
Approximately 2.5 for soda-lime glass, meaning it weighs 2.5 times as much as an equal volume of water. This value is used to estimate panel weights and buoyancy.

**What is the unit weight of glass?**
About 24.5 kN/m³ (156 lb/ft³) for soda-lime. A 6mm glass panel weighs roughly 15 kg/m² — a common number for glazing dead-load calculations.

**Why is glass strong in compression but weak in tension?**
Glass has ~20x higher compressive strength (900-1000 MPa) than tensile strength (30-50 MPa annealed). Tensile failure is driven by surface microcracks that propagate under tension (Griffith fracture); tempering puts the surface in compression to suppress crack opening and raise effective strength to 120-200 MPa.

**Is glass heavier than aluminum?**
Slightly: soda-lime glass is 2500 kg/m³ vs aluminum 2700 kg/m³ — actually slightly lighter by volume. Glass feels heavier than aluminum only in bulk because products are usually thicker.

**How do I convert kg/m³ to lb/ft³ for glass?**
Multiply by 0.06243: 2500 × 0.06243 = 156 lb/ft³. Conversely, multiply lb/ft³ by 16.018 to get kg/m³.

**Is glass stronger than acrylic?**
In compression, yes — glass reaches 900-1000 MPa vs acrylic's ~80 MPa. But in practical bending and impact, acrylic (PMMA) is tougher: it yields and deforms rather than shattering, has roughly 10x higher impact resistance, and is about half the density (1190 kg/m³). Glass wins on hardness, scratch resistance, chemical inertness, and UV stability; acrylic wins on impact safety, weight, and formability. Choose glass for optics, chemical service, and scratch-critical glazing; acrylic for impact-prone, lightweight applications.

**What is soda-lime glass made of?**
Soda-lime glass is the most common glass (~90% of all production), composed of approximately 70-75% silica (SiO₂), 12-15% soda (Na₂O), and 8-12% lime (CaO). This simple, low-cost composition is why it dominates windows, bottles, jars, and light bulbs. The same base chemistry is modified for special service — adding boron oxide gives borosilicate (Pyrex), while nearly pure silica gives fused silica/quartz glass for high-temperature and optical applications.

**What is the glass transition temperature of glass?**
For soda-lime glass the glass transition (T_g) is about 520-560°C. Below T_g, glass behaves as a rigid amorphous solid; above it, it gradually softens into a viscous liquid rather than melting sharply. The softening point (~700°C) is where glass deforms under its own weight, and practical forming and blowing are done above 700°C. This temperature-dependent viscosity, not a distinct melting point, is what makes glass so formable.

**What is the density of borosilicate glass?**
Borosilicate glass (Pyrex, Kimax) has a density of 2,230 kg/m³ (2.23 g/cm³) at room temperature, about 11% lighter than soda-lime glass at 2,500 kg/m³. The lower value comes from boron oxide replacing part of the soda and lime flux in the silica network, which also drops the Young's modulus from 70 GPa to 64 GPa and the thermal expansion from 8-9 down to 3.2 × 10⁻⁶/°C. For weight takeoffs, a 6 mm borosilicate pane is roughly 13.4 kg/m² against 15 kg/m² for the same thickness in soda-lime.

**What is the density of tempered glass?**
Tempered (toughened) glass has a density of 2,500 kg/m³, identical to annealed soda-lime glass, because tempering is a thermal quench that locks the surface into compression without changing the glass composition. Density therefore cannot be used to identify tempered glass, and a tempered panel weighs exactly what an annealed panel of the same thickness weighs — 6 mm is 15 kg/m², 10 mm is 25 kg/m². What changes is strength: design tensile strength rises from 30-50 MPa to 120-200 MPa, and the pane breaks into small blunt fragments instead of shards.

**What is the compressive strength of glass?**
Soda-lime and borosilicate glass reach 900-1,000 MPa in compression, and fused silica about 1,100 MPa — roughly 20 to 30 times the tensile strength of the same material. That figure is real but rarely governs design: glazing fails in tension, where surface microcracks open at only 30-50 MPa (annealed) or 120-200 MPa (tempered). Compressive strength becomes the governing value only in direct bearing applications such as glass columns, glass fins, or bearing pads, where allowable contact stress is further limited by edge condition and the risk of local crushing.

## Summary


Soda-lime glass (density 2500 kg/m³) is the least expensive, most common glass — transparent, chemically inert, and easily formed. Its engineering use is governed by its brittle nature and flaw-controlled tensile strength (30-50 MPa annealed, 120-200 MPa tempered). Glass is strong in compression but vulnerable to tensile stress from surface flaws, impact, and thermal shock. Always use borosilicate for sight glasses or thermal shock service, tempered/laminated for safety-critical applications, and respect its brittle fracture design rules.
