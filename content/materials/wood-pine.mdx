## Overview

Pine is the most commercially important softwood genus (Pinus), comprising ~120 species worldwide and the dominant construction lumber in North America and much of Europe. Pine is lighter, softer, and less dense than hardwoods like oak, but grows fast, machines easily, takes nails without splitting, and is well-suited to structural framing, plywood, and mass-market construction. Density varies widely by species and growing conditions.

<Formula>ρ = 350-650 kg/m³ at 12% moisture content; commercial construction pine (Southern Yellow Pine) ~500-550 kg/m³; Eastern White Pine ~350-400 kg/m³</Formula>

<Callout type="info" title="Softwood vs Hardwood" severity="info">
"Softwood" and "hardwood" are botanical classifications (gymnosperm vs angiosperm), not direct measures of hardness. Longleaf southern yellow pine (Janka ~4000N) is harder than many hardwoods, while balsa (a hardwood) is one of the lightest woods. Pine density varies from 350 (white pine) to 650 kg/m³ (dense southern pine) — choose the grade for the load.
</Callout>

## Key Properties

- **Fast growth**: Pine plantations reach harvest size in 20-40 years (vs 80-150 for oak); widely available and affordable
- **Workability**: Machines, saws, nails, and screws easily; less likely to split than hardwoods (pre-drilling rarely needed near edges)
- **Dimensional stability**: moderate movement with moisture changes; more stable than oak tangentially
- **Appearance**: Light color (pale yellow to light brown), prominent resin canals, visible growth rings
- **Natural durability**: Non-durable to moderately durable; sapwood susceptible to fungal decay and insect attack
- **Treatability**: Most species take preservative treatment well (critical for exterior/ground contact use)
- **Knots**: Natural knots from branches; graded for structural use by knot size/frequency

## Standard Construction Lumber Grading

### Appearance/Stress Grades
- **Select Structural**: Highest quality, minimal knots, highest allowable stresses
- **#1 & #2 Common**: Standard for framing; knots and imperfections within limits; #2 is most common
- **#3 & Utility**: Lower grades for temporary/non-critical bracing

## Applications

### Structural Construction (dominant use)
- House framing: studs, plates, joists, rafters, trusses (SPF — Spruce-Pine-Fir in North America; Douglas Fir-South)
- Glue-laminated beams (glulam) for long spans
- Engineered wood products: LVL (Laminated Veneer Lumber), I-joists (pine flanges with OSB/plywood web)
- Roof trusses and floor trusses
- Concrete formwork (lower grades, often reused)

### Panel Products
- Plywood (pine veneers) — sheathing, subfloor, siding
- OSB (oriented strand board) — strands compressed with resin; cheaper than plywood; dominant structural sheathing

### Treated Wood
- Pressure-treated pine: decking, landscape timbers, fence posts, retaining walls, sill plates
- CCA (older), ACQ, MCQ copper-based preservatives
- Fire-retardant treated for interior commercial framing

### Industrial & Paper
- Pulp and paper production (major use globally; kraft process)
- Pallets, crates, shipping containers
- Utility poles (treated Douglas Fir or Southern Yellow Pine)
- Railroad ties (cross-ties)

### Furniture & Joinery
- Less common for high-end furniture (soft, easily dented)
- Used for rustic/country furniture (white pine, knotty pine paneling)
- Paint-grade interior trim and molding

<Callout type="warning" title="Not Durable Outdoors Without Treatment" severity="warning">
Pine sapwood (which comprises most commercial lumber) is not naturally decay resistant — it will rot in 2-5 years in ground contact. Always use pressure-preservative-treated pine for exterior, ground contact, or damp locations. For naturally durable outdoor wood, use cedar, redwood, white oak, or tropical hardwoods.
</Callout>

## Summary

Pine (typical 500 kg/m³, varying 350-650 by species) is the world's dominant construction softwood — fast-growing, affordable, easily worked, and strong for its weight. It is the standard material for residential framing, engineered wood products (LVL, glulam, OSB), plywood, and pulp. Pine lacks natural decay resistance and must be pressure-treated for exterior or ground contact. For structural design, species grade tables specify allowable stresses; Southern Yellow Pine is the strongest common pine species.
